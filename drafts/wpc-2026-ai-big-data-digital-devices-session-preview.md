In about five weeks, Phoenix will host the World Parkinson Congress — one of the few gatherings where clinicians, researchers, people living with Parkinson's disease, and the scientists working on the next generation of tools all share the same room at the same time. That intersection is part of what makes WPC different, and it is the reason the conversations that happen there tend to feel more grounded than the ones that happen at disease-specific research conferences.

I will be presenting in MCS2, a session on artificial intelligence, big data, and digital devices in Parkinson's care. The session is organized around three talks that cover the full arc of where the field is heading — from how AI is beginning to reshape clinical practice, to what it actually takes to translate large datasets into decisions that benefit individual patients, to where digital endpoints stand as tools for measuring what matters in clinical trials.

I am looking forward to it in the specific way that only a well-constructed session makes possible. The three of us on the program are coming at the question from different angles, and the moderators — Diane Stephenson and Camille Carroll — have built a track record of sessions that generate the kind of back-and-forth that continues in the hallway afterward.

---

**The session:**

*MCS2 — Artificial Intelligence, Big Data, and Digital Devices in Parkinson's Care*

Moderated by Diane Stephenson (USA) and Camille Carroll (UK)

The three talks:

Bill Marks (USA) opens the session by examining how AI is already beginning to change Parkinson's clinical care — not as a future possibility but as a present-tense shift in how assessment and decision support are being built into clinical workflows. This is the implementation question: not whether AI can perform a task in a research setting, but what happens when it enters the clinic.

My talk, *From Big Data to Personalized Care*, makes the case that the field has a translation problem more than a data problem. The datasets exist. The computational approaches exist. What has not yet been solved is the architecture of inference — how you move from patterns in populations to decisions that are useful for a specific patient, in a specific moment, with the specific information a clinician actually has available. That is the gap I want to spend twenty minutes on.

Anat Mirelman (Israel) closes the session with an honest assessment of digital endpoints — where they have delivered in clinical research, where the evidence is still developing, and what it will take for wearable-generated data to earn the same confidence as outcomes that trial sponsors and regulators have trusted for decades.

Together the three talks trace a throughline from promise to practice. AI shows up early with real capability. The data exists at scale. The measurement tools are becoming sophisticated. The hard part — and the part I think will define the next five years — is closing the distance between what the field knows and what actually reaches the patient.

---

The session's learning objectives reflect that framing: how AI can be used in diagnosis and management, what role big data plays in improving outcomes, and what digital devices can and cannot do in the context of clinical trials. Those three objectives map almost exactly to the three talks, which makes this one of the more coherent session designs I have been part of.

If you are coming to Phoenix, the session is worth your time regardless of where you sit on the optimism-skepticism spectrum about these technologies. The goal is not to declare any of this solved. The goal is to map where we actually are — carefully, with reference to evidence — and to be honest about what the next steps look like.

I am genuinely looking forward to the conversation. More on the specific argument I will be making in Phoenix is in [the essay I published earlier this month](/writing/from-big-data-to-personalized-care-translation-problem).
