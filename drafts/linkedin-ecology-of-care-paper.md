For every 1,000 people living with diagnosed Parkinson's in the US, about 10 are in a structured research cohort or expert-center registry.

Much of what my field knows about this disease is learned from those 10.

We then apply much of that knowledge to the other 990, who are seen in primary care, in hospitals, in long-term care, often by clinicians they reach once a year or less.

In effect, the pyramid of care and the pyramid of evidence tend to point in opposite directions: we build much of our knowledge at the narrow tip and apply it across the wide base.

That has been workable while the front door to Parkinson's care stayed narrow. Wearables and blood-based biomarkers are now widening it, pushing more people into the pathway earlier. So one of the most useful roles for AI here may be helping carry specialist-level judgment outward, toward where patients actually are, as much as sharpening it further at the apex.

I made this case at the World Parkinson Congress in Phoenix. I've now written it up, alongside the figure and what it means for how we build and evaluate these tools.
