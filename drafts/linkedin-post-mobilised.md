Clinical trials in Parkinson's disease measure how patients perform during a 30-minute clinic visit.

But PD is a disease lived across thousands of waking hours: in how far someone walks to the kitchen, how often they stop, how their stride shortens between medication doses.

The Mobilise-D consortium, including Alison Yarnall, Lynn Rochester, Walter Maetzler, Alice Nieuwboer, Jeffrey Hausdorff, Anat Mirelman, and many others, just published one of the most careful real-world mobility characterizations in PD to date.

601 people with Parkinson's disease. 232 healthy controls. Five European sites. A single wearable sensor on the lower back worn continuously for seven days.

24 digital mobility outcomes. 20 of 24 showed significant differences between PD and controls.

The pattern: PD participants walked 1.35 hours per day vs. 1.87 in controls. Fewer steps (7,733 vs. 10,563 per day). Fewer walking bouts, and shorter ones. Higher cadence (92 vs. 85 steps/minute), reflecting the rapid, shortened stride of Parkinsonian gait.

Two findings stood out to me.

First: in shorter walking bouts, PD patients walked faster than controls (0.74 vs. 0.70 m/s). The authors explain this as compensatory power bursts to overcome bradykinesia at movement initiation, an effect that disappears in longer bouts. Walking speed in brief episodes may reflect motor compensation more than impairment.

Second: bout-to-bout variability was lower in PD, not higher. Healthy walkers naturally adapt their gait to context. PD patients showed more rigid, stereotyped patterns between bouts. Lower variability here signals reduced adaptability, not greater precision.

The disease stage analysis is where the trial relevance becomes clear. Significant separation between healthy controls and mild PD (H&Y I-II), and between mild and moderate (H&Y III), across walking activity, rhythm, and variability domains. That graded sensitivity is what a disease-modifying therapy trial endpoint needs to demonstrate.

This is cross-sectional data. It shows DMOs can discriminate across groups and stages, not yet that they track treatment-related change. That longitudinal evidence is what the consortium is building toward. The FDA and EMA are engaged with this data as part of formal qualification pathways for these measures as trial endpoints.

Gait is what patients with Parkinson's disease tell us bothers them most. The Mobilise-D consortium is building the rigorous foundation to measure it where it actually happens.
